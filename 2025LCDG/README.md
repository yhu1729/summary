### Summary

This review develops the physics and experimental status of beam-driven plasma-wakefield acceleration, in which a relativistic charged-particle bunch excites a plasma wave that accelerates a trailing bunch at gradients reaching roughly $100\,\mathrm{GV/m}$. It treats linear wakes, nonlinear blowout and suck-in regimes, transformer-ratio enhancement, hollow channels, driver matching and depletion, and the longitudinal and transverse evolution of the accelerated bunch. Beam loading can flatten the accelerating field and combine small energy spread with efficient energy transfer, while ion motion, scattering, hosing, mismatch, and alignment errors threaten emittance. The review compares electron, positron, and self-modulated proton drivers; surveys internal injection, plasma recovery, multistage coupling, numerical methods, facilities, and landmark experiments; and assesses collider and free-electron-laser concepts. Experiments have separately demonstrated large energy gain, substantial efficiency, energy-spread control, and emittance preservation. They have not yet combined application-level beam quality, charge, efficiency, repetition rate, and multistage operation. Positron acceleration, high-average-power plasma sources, compact achromatic staging, instability control, and predictive long-distance simulation therefore remain central challenges.

### Contributions

1. Unifies linear and nonlinear wake excitation with driver propagation and trailing-bunch dynamics in one beam-to-beam energy-transfer framework.
2. Relates beam loading, transformer ratio, depletion, matching, and focusing to the competing goals of high efficiency and preserved energy spread and emittance.
3. Compares electron, positron, and proton-driven regimes, including hollow channels, positron-specific plasma response, and self-modulation of long proton bunches.
4. Surveys internal-injection schemes, plasma recovery, multistage coupling, simulation strategies, experimental facilities, and major results across four decades.
5. Quantifies the gap between individually demonstrated performance and the simultaneous beam quality, power, efficiency, stability, and staging required by colliders and free-electron lasers.
