### Summary

This study demonstrates extreme-ultraviolet free-electron-laser amplification driven by a compact laser wakefield accelerator. A $0.8\,\mathrm{J}$ laser, shock-front injection, a stabilized supersonic gas jet, and laser-wavefront filtering generate reproducible monoenergetic electron beams approaching $400\,\mathrm{MeV}$ with sub-1% energy spread. The aperture mask halves wavefront fluctuations and improves electron pointing, while a nozzle stilling chamber suppresses gas-density and shock-position variation. Controlled beam loading and wakefield phase rotation minimize energy spread near a $20\,\mathrm{pC}$ bunch charge. Start-to-end particle-in-cell, beam-transport, and FEL simulations reproduce the measured diagnostics. Transporting the beam through a two-meter undulator produces radiation near $40\,\mathrm{nm}$ with approximately twentyfold nonlinear gain; spectral broadening, $K$-dependence, and suppression after emittance degradation distinguish amplification from spontaneous emission. Gain remains fluctuation-sensitive, especially to laser and beam pointing stability, but the experiment establishes a route toward compact femtosecond XUV and x-ray light sources.

### Contributions

1. Demonstrated approximately twentyfold XUV FEL gain from a laser-wakefield-accelerated beam.
2. Stabilized injection with a sharp density down-ramp and a long-stilling-chamber gas nozzle.
3. Improved laser wavefront and electron pointing using a simple movable aperture mask.
4. Tuned beam loading and phase rotation to obtain narrow-spread monoenergetic beams.
5. Validated the accelerator-to-undulator experiment with a start-to-end simulation chain.
