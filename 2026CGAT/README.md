### Summary

The authors report experimental evidence for directional, high-energy muons produced by a petawatt laser-wakefield accelerator. A high-charge GeV electron beam struck a 2 cm lead converter, where Bethe-Heitler pair production generated muons amid much larger electron, positron, and photon backgrounds. Two dipole magnets selected and transported charged particles through a shielded path to a Timepix3 detector, whose pixel tracks recorded both deposited energy and arrival time. Track-level likelihood analysis, compared with Monte Carlo modeling, gave a $(99.1\pm0.5)\%$ confidence that at least one detected event was a muon rather than noise. Measurements with thinner converters served as controls and produced likelihood distributions consistent with leptonic background. Simulations reproduced the observed signal and support scaling to higher-charge, higher-energy drivers. For a 10 Hz petawatt system, the study projects that suitable transport and shielding could deliver roughly $10^4$ muons per second onto square-centimeter areas. This establishes a compact route toward directed laser-driven muon beams for radiography and other applications.

### Contributions

1. Produced experimental evidence of directional high-energy muons from a laser-wakefield accelerator.
2. Combined magnetic transport, heavy shielding, and pixelated track detection to isolate muons from intense secondary-particle noise.
3. Quantified muon detection at $(99.1\pm0.5)\%$ confidence using track likelihoods and numerical modeling.
4. Used thinner-converter control shots to show that the corresponding tracks were consistent with background.
5. Projected a path to approximately $10^4$ guided muons per second with a 10 Hz petawatt laser.
