# 2026BC2

## Claude (September 2026)

### Summary

This review surveys machine-learning (ML) closures for plasma fluid models, which truncate the velocity-moment hierarchy of the collisionless Vlasov equation by expressing the highest retained moment through lower-order ones. It first summarizes analytic closures, namely adiabatic models extending the Chew–Goldberger–Low closure, Landau-fluid closures descended from the Hammett–Perkins (HP) closure, and gyrofluid models, and then introduces neural networks, physics-informed neural networks (PINNs), neural operators, and equation discovery by sparse or symbolic regression. The twenty ML studies in its summary table are grouped by training data (analytic closures or kinetic simulations) or as equation discovery, and by whether the closure was tested offline against reference data or online inside a time-evolving fluid solver; only five were tested online. The authors conclude that most work consists of one-dimensional proof-of-concept studies, mainly of Landau damping; that a Fourier neural operator (FNO) closure run online reproduced linear and non-linear Landau damping better than HP; and that off-diagonal pressure-tensor components are poorly captured by both neural-network and sparse-regression methods. Stated open problems are extrapolation beyond the training parameter range, numerical stability in multidimensional large-scale simulations, handling different phenomena and boundary conditions without retraining, and the cost of kinetic training data.

### Contributions

1. Tabulated the analytic adiabatic, Landau-fluid, and gyrofluid closures by closure class, target regime, and limitation, and identified three limitations they share: finite-Larmor-radius orderings that break at sub-gyroradius scales and weak guide field, Padé fits to the linear response that miss the suppressed phase mixing of turbulence, and near-Maxwellian expansions that fail in reconnection exhausts, shocks, and driven turbulence.
2. Used the offline/online distinction to single out the singular-value-decomposition closure of Shukla et al., trained at 3 of 35 parameter points with about 8% online error, whose online run far from its training point jumped spuriously to a higher transport level that offline scoring could not detect, and noted that the online neural-network closures of Wang et al., Huang et al., and Joglekar & Thomas all treat only one-dimensional Landau damping.
3. Contrasted the methods' practical limitations: PINNs that learn the whole fluid system (Qin et al.) give an implicit closure that cannot be moved into an independent solver, neural operators need more data and do not guarantee accuracy at finer resolutions, and whether the FNO's 10.8% error against 66.7% for a multilayer perceptron (Wei et al.) reflects the Fourier representation or model capacity is left open until operators are tested on closures not naturally expressed in Fourier space.
4. Identified that general symbolic regression and uncertainty quantification have not yet been applied to plasma closures, and that learned closures are rarely checked for energy and momentum conservation, Galilean or Lorentz invariance, entropy production, hyperbolicity of the closed system, and spurious growing modes.
5. Recommended a two-stage workflow in which a neural network first sets an accuracy baseline and sparse regression, or symbolic regression when the library fails, then seeks an interpretable form (after Ingelsten et al.), together with symmetry-based data augmentation, end-to-end training through differentiable solvers, locally selected specialized closures, pooled community kinetic data, and satellite observations as training data.

### Comments

- Fig. 1 caption: the figure is "Reproduced from Tóth et al. (2016), J. Geophys. Res. Space Phys. 122, 10,336-10,363, Fig. 2", but the reference list gives Tóth et al. (2016) as the Ganymede-magnetosphere article in volume 121 (2), 1273–1293, so the cited source of the figure is unresolved.
- Section 1.2.3: the small parameter $\epsilon$ is described as, among others, "the ratio of perpendicular fluctuation scale to the Larmor radius", which conflicts with the ordering given in the same passage, $\epsilon\sim k_\parallel/k_\perp$ and $k_\perp\rho\sim1$; the formula indicates that the intended ratio is that of the perpendicular to the parallel fluctuation scale.
- Section 1.2.3: Passot et al. (2018) are said to assume "anisotropic turbulence $k_\perp \ll k_\parallel$", which reverses the ordering $k_\parallel \ll k_\perp$ stated earlier in the same section for the gyrokinetic models from which gyrofluid models are derived; the intended reading is evidently $k_\parallel \ll k_\perp$.
- Table 1: the caption defines "5-moment (scalar pressure)", as does Section 1.2, yet Chew et al. (1956), Abraham-Shrauner (1967, 1968, 1973), and Le et al. (2009) are classed as "5-moment" although their own entries and Eqs. (1.18)–(1.21), (1.23), and (1.24) describe separate pressures $p_\parallel$ and $p_\perp$.
- Section 1.3: Maulik et al. (2020) is cited with Wang et al. (2020) for surrogate closures "integrated ... into fluid simulations, demonstrating that neural network closures can be coupled to fluid solvers and run stably in time", but Section 3.1 states that this study "was conducted entirely offline" and Table 2 lists it as offline.
- Fig. 6 caption: "Orange circles denote training-set error and blue triangles denote test-set error", but the legend assigns both a circle and a triangle to each set, orange to "train set" and blue to "test sets", and the plot switches from circles to triangles beyond five terms, so colour, not shape, distinguishes the sets.
- Section 3.3.3: "only Donaghy & Germaschewski (2023) has applied sparse regression to a two-dimensional problem" conflicts with Section 3.3.1 and Table 2, where McGrae-Menge et al. (2026) apply symmetry-augmented sparse regression to "two-dimensional PIC simulations of magnetic reconnection".
- Table 2: Liu et al. (2022) is listed under "Section 3.2: Kinetic simulation training data" with target "Implicit fluid closure", but the study is discussed in Section 3.3.2 as PINN-based equation discovery that yields the explicit heat-flux closure of Eq. (3.9), whereas Section 3.4 states that the table is organised by subsection.
- Section 4.1: "Ma et al. (2020) showed that several architectures could reproduce the non-linear Hammett-Perkins heat flux", but the HP closure of Eqs. (1.25) and (3.1) is linear in $\tilde{T}$ and Table 1 calls it "limited to linear, electrostatic regime"; Section 1.3 describes the same result as learning "the non-local Hammett-Perkins closure", evidently the intended word.
