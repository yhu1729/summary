## Claude (September 2026)

### Summary

The authors present H-NESSi, an open-source C++ library solving the Kadanoff–Baym equations (KBE), integro-differential equations of motion for two-time nonequilibrium Green's functions. Earlier solvers on dense two-time grids cost $\mathcal{O}(T^3)$ operations and $\mathcal{O}(T^2)$ memory for $T$ timesteps. Extending an earlier hierarchical off-diagonal low-rank (HODLR) scheme to multiorbital systems, H-NESSi partitions the lower-triangular retarded and lesser components into blocks stored as truncated singular value decompositions (TSVD) with tolerance $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{SVD}}$, updated row by row during high-order multistep integration, and samples imaginary-time functions on sparse discrete Lehmann representation nodes. The interface mirrors the NESSi package, users implement self-energies, and an MPI class redistributes data between time points and momenta. For a driven superconductor in dynamical mean-field theory, retained ranks grow as $N^{\alpha}$ with block size $N$ ($\alpha\approx0.5$ superconducting, $\approx0$ normal), the Dyson solver scales approximately as $T^{2+\alpha}$ against $T^3$ for NESSi, and SVD updates as $T^{2+2\alpha}$. For the two-dimensional Hubbard model in the second Born approximation, ranks saturate, integration scales as $N_t^{2.05}$, and equal memory allows about three times longer propagation. The authors state that physical accuracy depends on the user-provided self-energy, that $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{SVD}}>10^{-5}$ can amplify errors, and that integrating the density matrix along the diagonal can diverge in long steady states.

### Contributions

1. Kept the current row and the previous $k$ rows of $G^R$ and $G^<$, needed by the high-order integrators, uncompressed, compression thus lagging timestepping by $k+1$ steps, with read-write Eigen maps only there and read-only access elsewhere costing $\mathcal{O}(N_S^{\varepsilon})$ operations per value reconstructed from a TSVD block, left the Matsubara and mixed components uncompressed with imaginary time on DLR nodes, advised $N_t/2^{N_l}\approx10$ for the number of levels $N_l$, and compressed the $N_o^2$ orbital components separately, offline tests on simple multiorbital systems indicating no significant gain from combining orbital and time indices.
2. Integrated the density matrix along the diagonal, $\partial_t\rho(t)|_{t=T}=\xi i\partial_tG^<(t,T)+\xi i\partial_tG^<(T,t)$, at no extra cost (RHO_DIAGONAL): for a continuously driven two-band, two-site Hubbard model with $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{SVD}}=10^{-10}$ and $h=0.025$ to $0.0125$, $\operatorname{Re}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho(t)-\rho^M]$ stayed constant at about $10^{-10}$ to $10^{-7}$, whereas horizontal integration let it grow to about $3\times10^{-5}$ to $3\times10^{-3}$ and gave $\operatorname{Im}\operatorname{Tr}\rho(t)$ up to about $2\times10^{-5}$, a non-Hermiticity that forcing a Hermitian mean-field Hamiltonian would not remove.
3. Used MPI_Alltoallv in the mpi_comm class to pass from buffers holding all $t'$ at local momenta to buffers holding all momenta at a local subset of $t'$ and DLR nodes, reassigning $t'$ points at every timestep because their number grows, so that the time-local second-Born self-energy is evaluated in real space via FFTW at $\mathcal{O}(N_k\log N_k)$ rather than $\mathcal{O}(N_k^2)$ cost, with $G_{(k_x,k_y)}=G_{(k_x,-k_y)}$ reducing $L^2$ to $L(L/2+1)$ momenta and one dyson and dlr_info instance per OpenMP thread because neither class is thread-safe.
4. Fitted, for dynamical mean-field runs with $U=2$ and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{SVD}}=10^{-6}$ at $\beta=18$ (superconducting) and $\beta=1$ (normal), log-log slopes of cumulative wall time of 2.38 and 2.06 for the timestep solve and 3.03 and 2.12 for the SVD updates, against 3.03 for NESSi, and, for the Hubbard lattice with $L=128$, $\beta=50$, $U=0.5$, and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{SVD}}=10^{-12}$, propagated $N_t=6289$ time points in the memory that limits NESSi to $N_t=2048$.
5. Measured nearly linear MPI speedup up to 32 ranks on one node and OpenMP speedup near $N_{th}^{0.95}$ up to 16 threads for $L=48$, $N_t=2048$, and, for $L=64$, $N_t=16384$, found the fewest CPU hours on 2048 CPUs at 8 threads per rank (up to about 1.6 times more otherwise) with about 1 TB of memory, maximum $\varepsilon$-ranks ranging over the Brillouin zone from about 20 to 33 at $\beta=1$ and 40 to 50 at $\beta=5$, and memory savings near 100 for the largest blocks at $\beta=5$.

### Comments

- Abstract, Section 4.7, and Fig. 4(a): the abstract claims for the driven-superconductor and two-dimensional Hubbard benchmarks "asymptotic time complexity significantly below the cubic scaling of uncompressed approaches", but in the superconducting state the TSVD update step follows, per Section 4.7, "the expected scaling of $T^{2+2\alpha}$" with "$\alpha\approx0.5$ for the superconducting state", that is $T^3$; Fig. 4(a) fits it with slope 3.03, as for NESSi, and at the last step its cumulative time (about 60 h) exceeds that of the timestep solve (about 18 h, slope 2.38), so in the driven-superconductor benchmark only the Dyson solver and the normal state are shown to be sub-cubic; unresolved.
- Sections 2.4 and 3: the Integration class "supports discretization error scaling as $h^{k+1}$, where $1\le k\le5$ is a user-specified parameter", and $k$ is called "the integration order" (Sections 3.1 and 4.1), which gives five integrators, of first to fifth order in $k$ or second to sixth order in the error exponent, but Section 3 lists "first- to sixth-order integrators implemented in the integration class"; unresolved.
- Section 4.3, Eqs. (23)–(24): at $t'=0$ and $\tau=0$ the second contour arguments of the two components are the adjacent points $t_{0+}$ and $t_{0|}$ (Table 1, Fig. 1), both after every real-time point, so $G^{\rceil,HF}(t,0)$ must equal $G^{<,HF}(t,0)$, but (23) gives $i\exp[-i\epsilon(0_{|})t]\rho^M$ and (24) gives $\xi i\exp[-i\epsilon(0_{|})t]\rho^M$, which differ in sign for fermions ($\xi=-1$), the statistics of the example; since $\rho(t)=\xi iG^<(t,t)$ (Section 3.2) implies $G^<(t,t)=-\xi i\rho$, which (23) matches for fermions, the evident intended prefactor in (24) is $-\xi i$.
- Section 5.2, Eqs. (32)–(33): writing $\Sigma(z,z')=U^2G(z,z')G(z,z')G(z',z)$ as $-U^2$ times the Bubble2 product of $G$ with the Bubble1 product of $G$ and $G$, the rules of Section 3.3 reproduce $\Sigma^<$ of (33) but, through $C^M(\tau)=-A^M(\tau)B^M(-\tau)$ and $C^M(\tau)=-A^M(\tau)B^M(\tau)$, give $\Sigma^M(\tau)=-U^2G^M(\tau)G^M(\tau)G^M(-\tau)$, opposite in sign to (32), $\Sigma^M_{\mathbf r}(\tau)=U^2G^M_{\mathbf r}(\tau)G^M_{\mathbf r}(\tau)G^M_{-\mathbf r}(-\tau)$; with $G^M(-\tau)=\xi G^M(\beta-\tau)$ (Section 3.1) the derived form equals $U^2G^M(\tau)G^M(\tau)G^M(\beta-\tau)$ for fermions, which Sigma_mat in Appendix B implements for the analogous bubble with GM_reversed, so (32) evidently intends $U^2G^M_{\mathbf r}(\tau)G^M_{\mathbf r}(\tau)G^M_{-\mathbf r}(\beta-\tau)$, equivalently a leading minus sign.
- Section 5.2, Eq. (34): $\Sigma^{\lceil}_{\mathbf r}(t,\tau)=U^2G^{\lceil}_{\mathbf r}(t,\tau)G^{\lceil}_{\mathbf r}(t,\tau)G^{\rceil}_{-\mathbf r}(\tau,t)$ attaches the right-mixed symbol to the arguments $(t,\tau)$ and the left-mixed symbol to $(\tau,t)$, the reverse of Table 1, where $G^{\rceil}(t,\tau)$ is left-mixed and $G^{\lceil}(\tau,t)$ right-mixed; since H-NESSi stores and propagates the left-mixed component (Section 3.1, Eq. (7)) and the product that gives (33) yields $\Sigma^{\rceil}(t,\tau)=U^2G^{\rceil}(t,\tau)G^{\rceil}(t,\tau)G^{\lceil}(\tau,t)$, as Sigma_tstp in Appendix B implements, that is evidently the intended equation.
- Fig. 10 caption: the figure is described as "Performance scaling of the hybrid MPI/OpenMP implementation for a fixed total number of CPUs ($N_{CPU}=2048$)", and panel (c) as showing CPU hours "for two different simulation lengths", but the legend of panel (c) labels the second series "$N_t=8192$, $N_{CPU}=512$"; unresolved.
- Appendix A, Eq. (A.10): in $\omega_i=\sum_{j=0}^{i}b_{k+1-i}$ the summand does not depend on the summation index $j$, so the sum reduces to $(i+1)b_{k+1-i}$ rather than a cumulative sum of Adams–Moulton weights (for $k=2$, with $b_3,b_2,b_1=5/12,8/12,-1/12$, it gives $\omega_1=16/12$ and $\omega_2=-3/12$ instead of $13/12$ and $1$); the evident intended summand is $b_{k+1-j}$.
