# 2026K2

## Claude (September 2026)

### Summary

The author presents kalypsso, a block-structured adaptive mesh refinement (AMR) framework for compressible flow on CPUs and GPUs. The MPI-parallel, CPU-only forest-of-octrees library p4est refines, coarsens, 2:1-balances (neighbours differ by at most one level), and repartitions the mesh on the host, while solver data, one block of cells per leaf with an optional ghost halo, stay on the device for Kokkos kernels. Each AMR cycle downloads one refinement flag per leaf and uploads a hash table of 64-bit keys (tree, Morton index, level, outside-domain bits), from which kernels compute neighbour keys without searching; halos move by GPU-aware MPI. Finite-volume solvers for Euler (second-order MUSCL–Hancock), five-equation two-phase flow with THINC interface sharpening, and constrained-transport MHD are tested on shock-tube, Riemann, triple-point, shock–bubble, and blast-wave problems, the shock–bubble run matching measured wave speeds within 10%. On one GH200, 2D time-to-solution is shortest with $16^2$ blocks on GPU and $8^2$ on CPU, although GPU cell-update rates rise with block size, and 3D weak scaling retains about 80% node-to-node efficiency on 512 GPUs. As limitations, the author notes a single global time step, an AMR cycle taking up to 56.5% of GPU run time with $4^2$ blocks, rectangular domains only, and no communication–computation overlap.

### Contributions

1. Laid out the default 3D key as 15 tree-id bits (5 per direction, so up to $32^3$ octrees), 42 Morton-index bits, 4 level bits, and $d=3$ outside-status bits, and padded rectangular domains with outside blocks that reuse the key of their periodic image inside the box plus bits marking the directions of the faces through which they touch it, so that outside blocks, filled from the boundary condition, are otherwise handled like MPI ghost blocks and kernels access all neighbours alike.
2. Kept device hash tables for both the old and the new mesh so that a `Kokkos::parallel_scan` computes the memory offsets of the remapping, and let the block-data class wrapping a `Kokkos::View` reallocate only when a resize exceeds a capacity held 20% above the logical size (instead of the STL's doubling), since reallocating at every AMR cycle made runs on the CUDA backend about 20% slower; CPU backends showed no gain.
3. Measured on one GH200 (Table 1, $4096^2$ equivalent) best 2D AMR times of 41.2 s on the GPU and 164.7 s on the 72 ARM cores, against 109.1 s and 861.8 s for the uniform-grid code euler_kokkos with the same scheme, with GPU rates rising from 406 to 2678 million cell updates/s between $4^2$ and $512^2$ blocks, and in 3D ($512^3$, Table 2) found $8^3$ blocks best on both devices, at 151.9 s and 1407.0 s against 306.0 s and 2121.3 s.
4. Weak-scaled a replicated blast wave with one tree per GPU on up to 64 A100 and 512 GH200 GPUs; in 2D ($16^2$ blocks, levels 4–9) every run ended with 11.56% of the finest uniform grid's cells (7.7 million per process, about $4\times10^9$ on 512 GPUs), the AMR cycle every 20 steps took 11% of run time, and MPI communication took about 15% within one node, 33% at 8 processes, and 39% at 512.
5. Implemented constrained transport with face-averaged magnetic fields in a dedicated staggered array layout and HLLD fluxes, replacing the coarse EMF at nonconforming edges by the mean of the two fine EMFs as in AthenaK and prolongating with the Tóth–Roe operator, which kept $|\nabla\cdot\mathbf B|$ at machine precision ($10^{-15}$) at conforming edges and at most $10^{-11}$ near nonconforming faces; with this costlier solver the GPU AMR-cycle share fell below 10% for blocks above $8\times8$ (Table 4).

### Comments

- Abstract: with AMR activated and "comparable hardware resources", "a consistent $\times5$ CPU to GPU time to solution speed-up is obtained", but Section 5.2 reports "a $\times4$ speedup" between the best 2D times (164.7 s and 41.2 s in Table 1), the best 3D times in Table 2 give $1407.0/151.9\approx9.3$, and at equal block size the AMR runs give CPU-to-GPU time ratios of 3.1 to 6.6 in Table 1 and 8.0 to 9.3 in Table 2; unresolved.
- Section 4.3: "The ratio between the smallest and the largest block can be as high as $2^{5+14}$", counting the 5 tree-id bits per direction, and "the maximum ratio between largest and smallest mesh cell can be larger than $1\times10^6$" once the cells of the leaf blocks are included, but the octrees lie on a regular Cartesian grid (the brick connectivity of Section 2) and every leaf carries the same block of cells, so block sizes, and hence cell sizes, differ by at most $2^{14}$ (14 Morton bits per direction; $2^{13}$ with $l\in[0,13]$ in the Fig. 3 caption); both figures match instead the ratios of the domain extent, up to $2^5$ octrees per direction, to the finest block and cell.
- Fig. 8 caption: the solutions are "obtained at final time $t_f=0.6$", but Section 5.2 states that "Fig. 8(b) to (f) correspond to the final fluid state at time $t_f=0.8$", and Table 1, which reports these simulations, runs them "to $t_f=0.8$"; the evident intended value is 0.8.
- Table 1, $512^2$ row: this run has no AMR and a fixed 16.77 M cells, yet its CPU entries (282 Mcu/s, 1155.9 s) imply $3.26\times10^{11}$ cell updates, whereas its GPU entries (2678 Mcu/s, 139.8 s) and both euler_kokkos entries for the same uniform $4096^2$ problem to $t_f=0.8$ imply $3.74$–$3.75\times10^{11}$; the stated time would require about 324 Mcu/s; unresolved.
- Section 6.1: the mixture internal energy is defined as $\rho e=\sum_{i=0,1}\rho_ie_i$ next to the mixture density $\rho=\sum_{i=0,1}\rho_i\alpha_i$, but without volume fractions it counts the energy of a phase absent from a cell ($\alpha_i=0$) and does not reduce to $\rho e$ when $e_0=e_1=e$; the evident intended definition, consistent with the phasic densities $\alpha_i\rho_i$ of Eq. (6), is $\rho e=\sum_{i}\alpha_i\rho_ie_i$.
- Section 6.1.2: region 2 of the triple point, filled with the second fluid ($\alpha_0=0$, $\alpha_1=1$), is given "$\gamma_0=1.4$", whereas regions 1 and 3, filled with the first fluid, have $\gamma_0=1.5$ and Section 6.1 calls the $\gamma_i$ material constants; the evident intended value is $\gamma_1=1.4$.
- Section 6.1.3, Eq. (7): the post-shock state (1.6571, 0, −114.51, 0, 159060, 1.0) is said to follow from the Rankine–Hugoniot relations for the Mach 1.22 shock, but with the listed pre-shock air state (1.1839, 101325) and $\gamma_{\mathrm{air}}=1.4$ these give density 1.6295 and speed 115.5 m/s (only the pressure agrees), and $\alpha_0=0.28\rho_{\mathrm{bubble}}/\rho_{\mathrm{air}}$ with $\rho_{\mathrm{bubble}}=0.0614+0.1579$ gives 0.0519 rather than 0.051; all three stated values follow instead from a pre-shock density of 1.204; unresolved.
- Section 6.2, Eq. (11): the update is written for $B^{n+1}_{x,i+1/2,j,k}$ but uses the EMFs $E_{z,i-1/2,j\pm1/2,k}$ and $E_{y,i-1/2,j,k\pm1/2}$ on the edges of face $i-1/2$, and the preceding sentence announces "the explicit time evolution of $B^n_{x,i-/2,j,k}$"; the evident intended face index on the left-hand side is $i-1/2$.
- Section 6.2, Eq. (12): $p=0.1$ if $r<0.1$ and $p=10.0$ if $r\ge0.1$, but the text states that "a sphere of radius $r_c=0.1$ is at high pressure $P=10.0$ while the background pressure is $P=.1$", as the thermal-pressure colour bar of Fig. 17(b), spanning 0.091 to 1.0, also indicates; the two cases of Eq. (12) are evidently swapped.
- Section 6.2: Eq. (12) sets $\mathbf B=\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt2},\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\right]$, whereas the following text gives "$B_x=0.5$, $B_y=-0.5$ and $B_z=0$", which differs in magnitude and in the sign of $B_y$; unresolved.
- Section 6.2: the blast wave is said to have "a moderately low plasma beta of $\beta=\frac{2P}{B^2}=0.02$", but with the background pressure $P=0.1$ this formula gives $\beta=0.2$ for the field of Eq. (12) ($B^2=1$) and $\beta=0.4$ for the text's $B_x=0.5$, $B_y=-0.5$ ($B^2=0.5$); unresolved.
- Section 6.2: the text says that in Fig. 17 "The fluid density at final time $t_f=0.15$ is shown on the left, and the adaptive mesh levels on the right. We use a block size of $16\times16$ and AMR levels in range $[\![2,6]\!]$", but the Fig. 17 caption gives $t_f=0.2$, six panels, "Block size is $8^2$" and "$l\in[\![2,8]\!]$", consistent with the level colour bar of Fig. 17(c), which reaches 8; the final time is unresolved.
- Section 6.2: with both optima at $8^2$ blocks, AMR is said to bring "a $\times4$ speedup in the CPU case and $\times2.66$ speedup in the GPU case" over the full Cartesian resolution, but the $8^2$ row of Table 4 against the euler_kokkos row (165.8 s and 23.8 s against 801.3 s and 67.4 s) gives 4.8 and 2.8, whereas 2.66 matches the $16^2$ row ($67.4/25.2\approx2.67$); unresolved.
- Section 7: small blocks are said to be "usully associated to higher sparsity index, hence smaller mesh for a given space resolution", but Section 4.4 defines lower sparsity indices as meshes with fewer cells, and Section 5.2 and Table 1 show smaller blocks giving lower indices (10.6% at $4^2$ against 43.3% at $64^2$ at final time); the evident intended word is "lower".
