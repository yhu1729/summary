### Summary

Flying-focus laser pulses combine spatiotemporal coupling with an axiparabola to create a quasi-Bessel intensity peak whose axial velocity can be tuned, potentially preventing electrons from outrunning a laser wakefield. This work directly images the resulting plasma structures with femtosecond relativistic electron microscopy and reproduces them using linked optical, particle-in-cell, and probe-beam simulations. Repeated measurements establish that the observed V-shaped, off-axis wake structure is stable rather than a single-shot artifact. Density scans show that increasing plasma density shortens the wake period, strengthens and nonlinearizes the longitudinal field, splits the on-axis driver, and changes the phase relation between on- and off-axis wakefields; the simulated field rises from 11 to 99 GV/m across the tested range. Ionization materially changes the structure in helium compared with a preionized plasma, whereas adding a small nitrogen fraction has little structural effect despite its relevance to electron injection. Simulations also show a sharp difference between focusing at the start of the gas plateau and focusing farther inside it. These results validate a diagnostic-and-simulation workflow for optimizing flying-focus wakefields, but the study characterizes wake structure rather than demonstrating dephasing-free electron acceleration.

### Contributions

1. Directly imaged stable flying-focus wakefield structures with femtosecond relativistic electron microscopy.
2. Reproduced the measured structures with coupled optical, particle-in-cell, and probe simulations.
3. Quantified density-dependent changes in wake period, field strength, nonlinearity, and off-axis phase.
4. Distinguished the structural effects of preionization, hydrogen, helium, and helium--nitrogen targets.
5. Identified the focal-line position relative to the gas plateau as an important control parameter.
