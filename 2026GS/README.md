### Summary

This work investigates how energetic ions can promote internal transport barriers through kinetic $E\times B$ staircase structures in tokamak plasmas. Using a reduced Vlasov model averaged over cyclotron and bounce or transit motion, the authors connect staircase formation to negative mass instability, which bunches particles when their precession frequency decreases with radial action. Linear analysis and nonlinear TERESA simulations identify propagating staircases as analogues of Bernstein–Greene–Kruskal equilibria: coherent structures sustained by particle trapping. Trapped-particle modes drive their growth through autoresonance, while pairwise merging produces a dominant toroidal $n=1$ structure alongside a zonal $n=0$ component. With a stronger energetic-ion population, phase synchronization and resonant energy transfer amplify low-frequency zonal flow. The staircase subsequently disappears as its sustaining resonance is lost, leaving suppressed turbulence and an internal transport barrier. A separate simulation distinguishes initial energetic-ion–trapped-electron resonance from later staircase-mediated amplification. The mechanism is demonstrated within the reduced electrostatic model; the energetic-particle threshold is not determined analytically, and the correspondence with Kuramoto synchronization is a formal analogy.

### Contributions

1. Extended negative mass instability to a tokamak particle-mode model, deriving a dispersion relation connecting instability and negative frequency shift to radially decreasing precession frequency.
2. Identified propagating kinetic staircases as BGK-like structures sustained by particle trapping, with phase mixing smoothing internal filaments and a separate Vlasov–Poisson calculation clarifying the phase-space-hole analogy.
3. Demonstrated formation of a dominant toroidal $n=1$ staircase through pairwise merging of smaller structures, alongside an axisymmetric zonal-flow component.
4. Showed staircase-mediated zonal-flow amplification and turbulence suppression with increased energetic-ion density, followed by staircase destruction and internal transport barrier formation.
5. Distinguished early energetic-ion–trapped-electron resonance from later staircase-mediated amplification in a dedicated simulation, using frequency sidebands to support the autoresonance interpretation.
